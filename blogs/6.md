# A Series for $\pi$ from the Semicircle

**Written by: Soroush Saberan and Claude**

I recently found [an old text conversation](/blogs/images/pi-series-text.png) from more than ten years ago, in which I wrote down a series for $\pi$. I have completely forgotten how I came up with the formula. All I remember is that it was inspired by the work in [A Bizarre Way Of Evaluating Infinite Series](https://soroush.io/#/my-thoughts/1). There, the game was: take a series, pretend it is the Taylor expansion of some unknown function, and hope that function is one you can evaluate. That recovered Euler's answer to the Basel problem, $\pi^{2}/6$.

I asked an AI what approach I might have taken, and this post is what it came up with. The same idea runs backwards. Start with a function whose value you already know, expand it, and read a series off the coefficients. The upper half of the unit circle is a natural place to try this, because the area underneath it is a quarter of $\pi$. The series that comes out is

$$
\pi = 4 - 4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{(2n+1)!},
$$

where the coefficients are built by three recurrences:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n+1} &= b_{n}a_{n}, & a_{1} &= 1, \\
b_{n+1} &= b_{n} + c_{n}, & b_{1} &= 3, \\
c_{n+1} &= c_{n} + 8, & c_{1} &= 12.
\end{aligned}
$$

The rest of this post is where those recurrences come from, and why the sum is $\pi$.

## The quarter disk

Take the area of the unit disk to be the definition of $\pi$. The upper right quarter is the region under $y=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ from $0$ to $1$, so

$$
\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{4}.
$$

Write $y(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$. This is an even function of a very simple kind: it satisfies $y(0)=1$ and the first-order equation

$$
(1-x^{2})y' + xy = 0.
$$

(The identity is just $y'=-x/y$, cleared of the denominator.) Evenness kills every odd derivative at the origin, so the Taylor series uses only even powers. Inside the open interval $(-1,1)$, where the two branch points at $x=\pm 1$ are still out of reach,

$$
y(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{2n}, \qquad c_{0}=1.
$$

## The coefficients

Differentiate the series and substitute it into the differential equation:

$$
y'=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2nc_{n}x^{2n-1},
$$

$$
(1-x^{2})y'=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2nc_{n}x^{2n-1}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2nc_{n}x^{2n+1},
$$

$$
xy=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{2n+1}.
$$

The coefficient of $x^{2m+1}$ has to vanish. The first sum contributes when $n=m+1$, the second when $n=m$ (and contributes nothing if $m=0$), and $xy$ contributes $c_{m}$. So

$$
2(m+1)c_{m+1}-(2m-1)c_{m}=0,
$$

which is the two-term rule

$$
c_{m+1}=\frac{2m-1}{2(m+1)}c_{m}.
$$

In particular $c_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}$. The ratios $c_{m+1}/c_{m}$ are rational, but the quantities that actually sit in the series for $\pi$ are the derivatives themselves, $y^{(2n)}(0)=(2n)!\,c_{n}$. Those grow much faster, and their growth is exactly the triple recurrence above.

Computing the first few even derivatives at $0$ makes the pattern visible before any closed form does:

| $n$ | $y^{(2n)}(0)$ | $a_{n}$ | $b_{n}=a_{n+1}/a_{n}$ | $c_{n}=b_{n+1}-b_{n}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $3$ | $12$ |
| $2$ | $-3$ | $3$ | $15$ | $20$ |
| $3$ | $-45$ | $45$ | $35$ | $28$ |
| $4$ | $-1575$ | $1575$ | $63$ | $36$ |

Here $a_{n}=-y^{(2n)}(0)$. Each $b_{n}$ is the ratio of one even derivative to the previous one, and each $c_{n}$ is the first difference of those ratios. The first differences themselves go up by $8$ every time: $20-12=8$, $28-20=8$, $36-28=8$. A constant second difference is the signature of a quadratic, so the three recurrences are just that table, written so that it continues forever. Solving them confirms the guess. From $c_{n+1}=c_{n}+8$ and $c_{1}=12$,

$$
c_{n}=8n+4.
$$

Then $b_{1}=3$ and $b_{n+1}=b_{n}+c_{n}$ give

$$
b_{n}=3+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(8k+4)=4n^{2}-1,
$$

and therefore $a_{n+1}=(4n^{2}-1)a_{n}$ with $a_{1}=1$. The claim connecting this back to the semicircle is

$$
(2n)!\,c_{n}=-a_{n}\qquad\text{for every }n\ge 1.
$$

The case $n=1$ is $2!\cdot(-\frac{1}{2})=-1=-a_{1}$. If it holds at $n$, the ratio for $c_{n}$ upgrades it to $n+1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2n+2)!\,c_{n+1}
&=(2n+2)(2n+1)\cdot\frac{2n-1}{2(n+1)}\cdot(2n)!\,c_{n} \\
&=(2n+1)(2n-1)\cdot(2n)!\,c_{n} \\
&=(4n^{2}-1)(-a_{n}) \\
&=-a_{n+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the Taylor series of the semicircle is

$$
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}, \qquad |x|<1.
$$

## Integrating the series

On any interval $[0,x]$ with $x<1$ the series converges uniformly, so it can be integrated term by term:

$$
\int_{0}^{x}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\,dt=x-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\frac{x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}.
$$

Now let $x$ increase to $1$. The integrand is continuous on the closed interval $[0,1]$, so the left side increases to the quarter-disk area $\pi/4$. On the right, every coefficient $a_{n}$ is positive, and for each fixed $x<1$ the sum is at most $x\le 1$. The series at $x=1$ therefore converges to some number at most $1$, and the two limits agree:

$$
\frac{\pi}{4}=1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{(2n+1)!}.
$$

Multiply through by $4$ and the stated formula drops out.

One sibling is worth writing down. The same identification of coefficients says that the series for $\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ itself, stopped before the integration, has general term $a_{n}/(2n)!$. That term is on the order of $n^{-3/2}$, so the series still converges at the endpoint $x=1$. Its sum there is the function value $\sqrt{1-1}=0$, which rearranges to

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{(2n)!}=1.
$$

The factorial in the series for $\pi$ is this one, with a single extra factor of $2n+1$ from the integral.

## A closed form for the general term

Since $4k^{2}-1=(2k-1)(2k+1)$, the product for $a_{n}$ splits into two strings of odd numbers:

$$
1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdots(2n-3)=\frac{(2n-2)!}{2^{n-1}(n-1)!}, \qquad 3\cdot 5\cdots(2n-1)=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}.
$$

The second string is $1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdots(2n-1)$ with the leading $1$ left off, so the two agree. For $n=1$ both products are empty and both sides equal $1$. Hence

$$
a_{n}=\frac{(2n-2)!\,(2n)!}{2^{2n-1}(n-1)!\,n!},
$$

and dividing by $(2n+1)!=(2n+1)(2n)!$ leaves

$$
\frac{a_{n}}{(2n+1)!}=\frac{(2n-2)!}{2^{2n-1}(n-1)!\,n!\,(2n+1)}.
$$

The central binomial coefficient produces the same expression. Since $4n^{2}-1=(2n-1)(2n+1)$ and $(2n)!=(2n)(2n-1)(2n-2)!$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}(4n^{2}-1)}
&=\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}(2n-1)(2n+1)} \\
&=\frac{2(2n-2)!}{4^{n}(n-1)!\,n!\,(2n+1)} \\
&=\frac{(2n-2)!}{2^{2n-1}(n-1)!\,n!\,(2n+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The two sides match, so the series is also

$$
\pi=4-4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^{n}(4n^{2}-1)}.
$$

This is the binomial series for $\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$, integrated once and evaluated at the endpoint. The central binomial coefficient grows like $4^{n}/\sqrt{\pi n}$, so the general term is about $1/(4\sqrt{\pi}\,n^{5/2})$. The series converges, but a partial sum has to be carried quite far before it looks like $\pi$.

Same trick as the Basel post, pointed the other way. There, integrating a geometric series manufactured a function, and the function evaluated to $\pi^{2}/6$. Here the function is the semicircle, the integral is $\pi/4$ by the area of the disk, and the Taylor coefficients are the series.
