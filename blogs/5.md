# Anthropic Argument for Continuous Subjective Experience

**Written by Soroush Saberan**

## The Initial Probabilistic Dilemma

Let us assume, for the sake of argument, the existence of an afterlife. We can then define two distinct periods of conscious existence:

1.  **$\text{Life}_1$**: The current, finite period of conscious existence, approximated as roughly 100 years.
2.  **$\text{Life}_2$**: A hypothetical subsequent period, assumed for this dilemma to be infinite or indefinitely long.

Let $T_{\text{Life}_1}$ be the duration of the current conscious existence, and $T_{\text{Life}_2}$ the duration of the hypothetical subsequent one. A uniform selection is defined for a finite duration, so take $T_{\text{Life}_2}$ finite and then let it grow. Selecting a moment uniformly from the conscious existence spanning the two periods,

$P(\text{moment is in } \text{Life}_1) = \frac{T_{\text{Life}_1}}{T_{\text{Life}_1} + T_{\text{Life}_2}}$.

If $T_{\text{Life}_2} \rightarrow \infty$, then $P(\text{moment is in } \text{Life}_1) \rightarrow 0$.

The observation this limit tells against is narrower than the bare fact of being conscious. If memory and identity are preserved into $\text{Life}_2$, a moment drawn from the combined existence almost certainly includes a memory of $\text{Life}_1$ and of the transition out of it. The present remembered history is finite and contains no such transition. That is the kind of moment the limit assigns a probability approaching $0$. An infinite $\text{Life}_2$ that preserves memory of $\text{Life}_1$, while the present remains inside $\text{Life}_1$, is therefore unlikely.

## The Exception: Unrecognized Continuation

That result uses the memory of the transition to recognize the present as the initial segment. If our current experience is actually some later $\text{Life}_i$, with $i > 1$, and memory is not preserved across the transition from $\text{Life}_{i-1}$, the present moment carries no such mark. Dilemma 1 then does not disconfirm the moment. It only disconfirms an infinite continuation that would have left a memory the present does not contain.

If memory and the capacity for subjective experience (sentience) are distinct, the cessation of a previous biological life and its associated memory functions would not necessarily entail the cessation of sentience. The "self" experiencing the current life would not recall any previous lives.

## The Anthropic Perspective on Time and Sentience

**The Second Probabilistic Dilemma**: What are the chances that time just so happens to be where it needs to be for you to be alive right now? If we assume you only have 1 life, then this single, finite period of existence becomes an extraordinarily improbable event when viewed against the backdrop of an infinite timeline.

Let $T_{\text{Life}_1}$ denote the duration of your current, finite period of conscious existence (as defined earlier, approximated as roughly 100 years). For this specific dilemma, we assume this $\text{Life}_1$ is the _only_ period of conscious existence you will ever have. Let $T_{\text{Timeline}}$ be a finite stretch of the timeline containing that life. Selecting a moment uniformly from it,

$P(\text{moment is in } \text{Life}_1) = \frac{T_{\text{Life}_1}}{T_{\text{Timeline}}}$.

If $T_{\text{Timeline}} \rightarrow \infty$, this probability tends to $0$. A single finite life occupies a vanishing fraction of an infinite timeline, and the present moment falls inside that life.

Sentience being required for an observation does not change the fraction. Any moment that is noticed is sentient, under one life or under many, so a sample restricted to sentient moments makes "this moment is sentient" certain either way. That sample is also a different reference class from the timeline, so it sets the ratio aside rather than evaluating it. Dilemma 2 is the timeline ratio. It counts against a single finite life while the present is treated as an ordinary moment of the timeline.

Later lives change the probability that the present is sentient at all when they lie on that same timeline and their combined share of it does not tend to $0$. The standard here is the same one the limit already uses: the share must not tend to $0$. It need not be most of the timeline. A life placed in a different universe does not add to the share of this one. Infinitely many finite lives do not add enough if the gaps grow so fast that the sentient share still tends to $0$. Then each particular life has probability approaching $0$, and so does the event that the present is sentient at all.

The view from inside the succession is a further claim, and it is the one about subjective continuity. A period with no sentience is not lived through. If a later life begins with no memory of the gap, no moment of experience presents the gap as elapsed time, however long that gap is. Subjectively there is a present, then a further present. Continuous subjective experience, as used here, means that sequence of presents. It does not by itself make the sentient share of the timeline large.

## Resolution: Continuous Sentience, Discontinuous Memory

Based on this line of anthropic reasoning:

1.  An infinite $\text{Life}_2$ that preserves memory of $\text{Life}_1$ puts almost every conscious moment after the transition. The present has a finite remembered history and no memory of a transition, so that picture is a poor fit. This casts doubt on an afterlife in which identity and memory carry straight through while the present is still the initial finite period.
2.  On the timeline sampling above, one finite life makes the present's sentience a probability approaching $0$. The fact that we can only observe while sentient does not repair it, because that fact holds for one life as well. A non-vanishing sentient share of the same timeline does repair it: being sentient at all then does not tend to probability $0$.
3.  A sequence of finite lives, with no memory carried from one to the next, can meet both constraints. There is no infinite memory-continuous run in which the present would be an atypical initial segment, which is what dilemma 1 asks for. When those lives together are not a vanishing share of the timeline, the present is not a solitary finite island either, which is what dilemma 2 asks for. Memory, and the identity tied to a particular $\text{Life}_i$, can end with that life. The gaps remain real on the timeline and absent from experience, so the subjective present continues across them. This is continuous subjective experience with discontinuous memory.

Or put more simply:

- $\text{Life}_1 \rightarrow \text{Life}_2$ where $T_{\text{Life}_2} \rightarrow \infty$ (with preserved memory and identity from $\text{Life}_1$) is a poor fit on dilemma 1. Almost every conscious moment would remember the transition, and the present moment does not.
- $\text{nothing} \rightarrow \text{Life}_1 \rightarrow \text{nothing}$ (where $\text{Life}_1$ is a single, isolated period of sentience on an infinite timeline) is a poor fit on dilemma 2. Its sentient share of the timeline tends to $0$.
- $\ldots \rightarrow \text{Life}_{i} \rightarrow \text{Life}_{i+1} \rightarrow \text{Life}_{i+2} \rightarrow \ldots$, where each $\text{Life}_{i}$ is finite and memory is not preserved between them, meets dilemma 1 because no life is an infinite memory-continuous run, and meets dilemma 2 when the lives together do not form a vanishing share of the timeline. Our current experience is one such $\text{Life}_i$. The gaps are unexperienced, so subjectively the present continues.

## Caveats

**Continuous sentience with discontinuous memory is one resolution among many.** The conclusion above is that a sequence of finite lives, with no memory carried from one to the next, can resolve both dilemmas. Each $\text{Life}_i$ is finite, so there is no infinite run of preserved memory for the first dilemma to rule out. Sentience is not confined to a single finite stretch of the timeline, and so long as the lives together do not form a vanishing share of it, the second dilemma does not arise either. That picture meets both constraints. The argument does not claim it is the only picture that does. A great many other hypotheses could resolve both dilemmas as well, and this post does not try to list them.

**Both dilemmas use an infinite form of the self-sampling assumption. The finite form has problems this one does not.**

The self-sampling assumption is usually stated for a finite reference class: the present is a random draw from a finite set of observers or moments. That finite form fails in ways that do not carry over to the infinite form used above.

Its conclusions do not follow from the sampling. Nick Bostrom formulated the assumption and defends it, but treats [the doomsday argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doomsday_argument) as inconclusive rather than as a result the assumption forces. That argument, from Brandon Carter and John Leslie, treats one person's place among everyone who will ever live as a random sample, and treats an early place as evidence that humanity does not have a very long future. J. Richard Gott's Copernican argument samples in the same way: the present moment is a random time in the lifespan of the phenomenon being observed, so how long it has already lasted is used to estimate how long it will last. Dennis Dieks and Ken Olum argue that this sampling does not justify those conclusions. Tomáš Kopf, Pavel Krtouš, and Don Page argue that the doomsday argument is inconclusive. Carlton Caves argues that Gott's rule, reading a future duration off the present age, is not valid in general. Joe Carlsmith has more recently argued that the assumption is the wrong rule for these problems. What the finite form is asked to deliver, a prediction of how long a life or a population still has left, is not something one random draw establishes.

It is also statistically uninformative, because the sample has size one. A single moment drawn from a finite duration leaves a very wide error bar on that duration. The present age is one draw, and one draw from a finite interval is compatible with total lengths that differ by large factors. Gott's own 95% interval for the remaining duration runs from one thirty-ninth of the age already observed to thirty-nine times that age. The finite form never pins its reference class down. What it returns is a loose range, not a determinate length.

The form I use avoids both of those defects, because the reference class is infinite and the claim is a limit rather than an estimate of a length. In the first dilemma the reference class is every moment of a person's conscious existence, with the later life taken to be endless. In the second it is every moment of the timeline, including moments with no sentience. Dilemma 2 depends on that wider class. The probabilities are the fraction of that class occupied by $\text{Life}_1$, $\frac{T_{\text{Life}_1}}{T_{\text{Life}_1} + T_{\text{Life}_2}}$ and $\frac{T_{\text{Life}_1}}{T_{\text{Timeline}}}$. There is no remaining lifetime to infer, and no future length of humanity to read off one person's place in it. A finite stretch occupies a vanishing fraction of an infinite whole, so the probability of finding the present moment there tends to $0$. There is no interval left to widen, because there is no finite length being estimated. This does not answer the further charge that self-sampling is the wrong rule. The defects above concern a remaining length inferred from one draw, and the limit above infers no such length.

At first glance, it might seem wrong to sample from an infinite reference class. That is the dartboard paradox: infinitely many points can be hit, each with probability 0, and at the limit the total is 0 times infinity. These dilemmas are different. They have two possibilities, one approaching probability 0 and the other approaching 100%, and those still add up to 100%. If the claim were that being alive has probability 0, the resolution above would fail too, because any one specific life still has probability 0. The difference is between being alive in one specific life and being alive at all. Being alive at all avoids tending to $0$ when the sentient share of the reference class does not tend to $0$. The succession secures that share when its lives lie on the timeline being sampled and are not so sparse that their combined fraction vanishes. Each of those lives can still be vanishingly rare.
